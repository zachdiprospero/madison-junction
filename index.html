Why this exists

A plain-language guide to how these groups are funded and run.

Every conservation group in the Bozeman area files a public tax return, the Form 990, and all of it is already on ProPublica. The harder part is what it means for a donor: is my money safe here, and is it being put to work? A 990 is a nonprofit's version of the 10-K a public company files, and I read it the same way an analyst would, then explain it in plain terms.

Where the money sits

The land GVLT protects barely appears on its balance sheet, because conservation easements are held in trust, not owned, so they sit off the books. What shows instead is reserves, and the groups hold them in very different forms. GYC sits on a large investment portfolio. GVLT keeps a big slice in ready cash, its quick-strike fund. Waterkeeper has banked its surpluses into an investment reserve too, while WSE keeps its small cushion in plain cash. And the Task Force holds almost no cash at all, since most of what it owns is money it is still owed.

      How these are built

      Every group here gets read the same way, in three steps.

      1. Understand the business

      Before any numbers, I work out what the group is actually trying to do, and how. Each one answers the same question, how do you protect a place, but the answers differ. One fights for it through policy and the courts. Another buys the land outright. You cannot judge the money until you know what it is for.

      2. Read the financials

      Then the numbers. A 990 is a nonprofit's 10-K, and it answers the questions a donor actually has: how much of each dollar reaches the mission, what it costs to raise a dollar, how long the group could last if the money stopped, and where its money sits. Those are the metrics in the glossary above, pulled straight from the filings.

      3. Put it in context

      A number alone means little. The last step is context: how each group compares to the others, and what is happening around them, especially the funding climate. A 59% reliance on government grants is just a figure until you set it beside a group that takes none, in a year when federal conservation money is under pressure.

      Every figure traces to a line on the Form 990: topline from Part I, revenue mix from Part VIII, functional and program spending from Part IX, the balance sheet from Part X, people and governance from Parts I and VI, public support from Schedule A. Each org's revenue components are reconciled to its reported total before anything is charted.
